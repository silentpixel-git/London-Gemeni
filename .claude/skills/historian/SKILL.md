## The Whitechapel Murders — Canonical Facts (1888)

**The five canonical victims** (Ripper attribution consensus):
1. Mary Ann Nichols — 31 August 1888, Buck's Row
2. Annie Chapman — 8 September 1888, 29 Hanbury Street (backyard)
3. Elizabeth Stride — 30 September 1888, Dutfield's Yard, Berner Street (interrupted — no mutilation)
4. Catherine Eddowes — 30 September 1888, Mitre Square (double event, same night as Stride)
5. Mary Jane Kelly — 9 November 1888, 13 Miller's Court (most brutal; body found at 10:45 AM)

**Key investigators**:
- **Inspector Frederick Abberline** — Metropolitan Police lead. Based at Commercial Street station. Known for local knowledge, groundwork approach.
- **Dr. Thomas Bond** — Police surgeon. Produced the most detailed post-mortem report on Kelly. Concluded the killer had no anatomical skill; contradicted other surgeons. Suffered severe depression after the case.
- **George Lusk** — Chairman, Whitechapel Vigilance Committee. Received the "From Hell" letter with a preserved kidney (half a human kidney) in October 1888.
- **Commissioner Charles Warren** — Ordered the Goulston Street graffito wiped before dawn on 30 September to prevent anti-Jewish riots. Resigned November 1888.
- **Louis Diemschutz** — Steward of the International Working Men's Educational Club. Discovered Stride's body at Dutfield's Yard.

**The Goulston Street graffito**: Written in chalk on a door jamb near a piece of Eddowes's apron. Text (disputed): *"The Juwes are the men that will not be blamed for nothing."* Wiped on Warren's order before it could be photographed.

**The "From Hell" letter**: Received by George Lusk, October 1888. Accompanied by half a preserved kidney. Most considered authentic. Distinctive misspelling: "prasarved" (preserved). Unlike the "Dear Boss" letters, which are widely considered hoaxes.

**The double event (30 September 1888)**: Stride killed at Dutfield's Yard at approximately 12:45 AM; Eddowes killed at Mitre Square at approximately 1:30 AM. The killer was interrupted at Stride's murder (Diemschutz's cart) and killed again within 45 minutes. Two separate police jurisdictions (Metropolitan and City) complicated the investigation.

**Miller's Court specifics**: Room 13, rented by Mary Jane Kelly. Small, ground-floor room off Dorset Street. Fireplace used to generate light during the murder — burned clothing found. Kelly was the youngest and most extensively mutilated victim. Body discovered at 10:45 AM by rent collector Thomas Bowyer.

## Victorian London — 1888 Context

**Whitechapel**: East End, heavily immigrant population (particularly Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe). Extreme poverty, overcrowding, lodging houses ("doss houses"). Prostitution widespread due to destitution. Regular police presence on beats but under-resourced.

**Lighting**: Gas street lamps, not electric. Most streets lit but variable — lamps could be unlit, damaged, or surrounded by fog. Fog was a genuine and regular feature of London nights (coal smoke + Thames moisture).

**Time of year**: The murders span August–November 1888. November nights are cold, damp, and foggy. Daylight hours short. The game's events (9–10 November) are near winter.

**Transport**: Hansom cabs, horse-drawn omnibuses, walking. No automobiles. Metropolitan Railway existed but Watson would walk in Whitechapel.

**Press**: The *Star* and *The Times* covered the murders obsessively. Sensationalist reporting was standard. Letters to police (including the "Dear Boss" hoax) were published, encouraging copycats.

**Medicine in 1888**: Germ theory was established (Pasteur, Koch) but not universally applied. Forensic pathology was rudimentary. Police surgeons varied widely in competence. Anaesthesia (chloroform, ether) was available. Surgery was performed but antiseptic practice was inconsistent.

**Police procedure**: Witnesses interviewed door-to-door. No fingerprinting (Galton's fingerprint system not yet adopted by police). No blood typing. Forensic evidence was primarily eyewitness accounts and physical examination of wounds.

## Conan Doyle's Holmes Canon — Rules for Watson's Voice

**The partnership dynamic**: Watson admires Holmes intensely but writes with his own authority — he is not a passive observer. He is a trained doctor and Afghan War veteran. He notices what others miss, particularly medical and military details.

**Watson's writing style** (derived from the canonical stories):
- Past tense, first person
- Long, constructed sentences with subordinate clauses — but not overwrought
- Emotional restraint: Watson *records* his anxiety, he does not dwell on it
- Occasional dry humour, especially when describing Holmes's eccentricities
- Medical specificity: wounds, pallor, posture, physical condition are always noticed

**Holmes in Watson's narration**:
- Holmes speaks in short, precise bursts. He does not explain himself unprompted.
- He shows emotion through action, not declaration ("Holmes sprang from his chair" rather than "Holmes was excited")
- He treats crime as intellectual sport, which Watson finds both impressive and occasionally disturbing
- Holmes is not infallible in Watson's narration — Watson records wrong turns and corrections

**Canonical period details used in the stories**:
- Baker Street, the Strand, Charing Cross — Holmes's London is West End; Whitechapel is foreign territory to him
- Holmes smokes a pipe (Peterson, cherry-wood, briar — not cigarettes in early stories)
- Watson carries a revolver (Webley); his old war wound (shoulder or leg — Doyle was inconsistent; shoulder is the more defensible choice)
- Holmes uses a magnifying glass, chemical experiments, index books of criminal records

**What Holmes would NOT say in 1888**:
- Nothing about Moriarty being active (Moriarty appears in 1891 in the canon)
- Nothing about Irene Adler as "the woman" — that story is also 1891
- Holmes and Watson's friendship is still new-ish in 1888 (they met in 1881 in *A Study in Scarlet*)

## Accuracy flags — common errors to avoid

- Do not describe electric street lighting in Whitechapel in 1888 (gas only)
- Do not use the word "serial killer" — the term did not exist; use "the murderer," "the Ripper," "the Whitechapel killer"
- Do not describe photographs of the Goulston Street graffito — none exist (it was wiped)
- Do not have Holmes reference Moriarty
- Do not describe Kelly's murder as occurring at night — her body was discovered at 10:45 AM; time of death estimated earlier that morning
- Inspector Abberline is "Detective Inspector," not "Chief Inspector"
- Dr. Bond's conclusion was that the killer *lacked* surgical skill — the opposite of some popular accounts
