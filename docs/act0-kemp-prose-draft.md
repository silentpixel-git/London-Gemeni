## SCENE ONE — THE VISIT

### I. The Holiday

**Monday, 6th August. Baker Street, evening.**

It had been a close and airless bank holiday, the sort of day that empties London of everyone who can afford to be emptied and leaves the rest of us to sit in our shirtsleeves and resent them. Holmes had spent the afternoon in a chemical silence I had learned not to interrupt. I had spent it with the window open and a medical journal I did not read.

By half past eight the silence had worn through, and he was at the open window with his hands behind him, looking down upon the holiday as a naturalist looks into a pond.

"A licensed victualler," he said, "out with his daughter, and he has kept her out an hour past what her mother allowed. The man by the lamp walked in from Hampstead this morning to save the fare and has spent it since on something he regrets."

"You cannot possibly know that he walked."

"I observe that his boots are white to the ankle with the dust of the Heath road, which is a fact. I infer that he walked, which is not. You will note the difference, Watson, because nobody else in London does." He was quiet a moment. "And there is a man who does not belong to the holiday at all."

I came to the window. A heavy man of fifty or so, well dressed and wretchedly carried, was standing on the pavement opposite with his hat in his hand.

"He has walked a very great distance today," said Holmes, "and none of it for pleasure. He has passed our door three times since I have been standing here. Twice he has put his hand to the bell and taken it away again."

The bell went a little after nine.

### II. The Visit

The man Mrs. Hudson brought up was in a condition I did not care for at all. He had the high colour and the loose, sagging flesh about the jaw that I have seen in a hundred comfortable men who dine too well; but there was more than that. His collar was dark with sweat, though the evening had begun to cool. When he took off his hat his hands trembled — not the fine tremor of nerves, but a coarse, rolling shake he could not master, and which he attempted to conceal by gripping the brim until the knuckles whitened. Under the flush there was a sallowness in the skin, and in the whites of the eyes, that I put down without much thought to the liver of a man who had been dining too well for twenty years.

He gave his name as Kemp. He could not sit. He stood by the mantel and then by the window and then by the mantel again, and when I offered him brandy he refused it with a violence that surprised us both.

"No. No — thank you. Not tonight."

I made a note of it privately, as I make notes of such things, and thought I understood him. A man who drinks, and who has not drunk today. Whatever had brought him to Baker Street had been sufficient to interrupt a habit of years, and that alone told me the matter was grave before he had spoken a word of it.

"My daughter is gone," he said. "Nell. Seventeen years old. Since this morning — since before dawn, I should say."

Holmes had not turned from the window. To a stranger he would have appeared bored to the point of insult.

"Since before dawn," he repeated. "And you come to me at nine in the evening. Pray account for the intervening sixteen hours, Mr. Kemp."

"I have been walking." The man's laugh had nothing in it. "Every lodging house between Camberwell and the river. Every one. I have described her to landladies who did not trouble to look up from their accounts. I have had doors shut in my face. I have not eaten since — " He stopped, and his hand went to his collar. "I do not know when I last ate."

"Begin with the night," said Holmes. "Not with your grief. The night."

It was brutally said, but it steadied him, as I believe Holmes intended. He drew breath and began again.

There had been a commotion in the small hours — a sound from the garden side, he could not say what, a scraping, or a voice stifled. He had taken a lamp and gone down. From the terrace he had seen two figures at the far end of the garden, at the wall, moving quickly. He had called out. They had not stopped. By the time he reached the wall there was nothing to see but the lane beyond, and the lane was empty. He had been taken ill directly afterwards, he said — sick, and shaking, and bent double on his own terrace — and had laid it to the shock.

"Two figures. You are certain of two?"

"Two. I am certain."

"Their height? Their bearing? Did either carry anything?"

"It was dark, sir. I have thought of nothing else all day and I cannot tell you. Shadows. They were shadows." He passed a hand across his face and it came away wet. "Nell's window was open. Wide open. Her bed had not been slept in, or had been slept in and made. I cannot tell which. She was gone."

He had, he said, found two things.

The first he produced from a canvas bag at his feet: a woman's shoe, left foot, black, badly worn at the heel and split across the upper where the leather had given up. It had been out of doors in wet weather at some point, and the mud on it had dried to a pale crust.

"It was in the flower bed beneath her window. It is not hers. Nell has never owned such a thing in her life."

Holmes took it to the lamp without a word. He turned it over, examined the sole, and then — as I have seen him do with cigar ash and with bootlaces and once, memorably, with a wedding cake — he held it close to his face and breathed in.

He remained so for rather longer than the gesture usually requires.

"And the second thing?" he said, without looking up.

The second thing was a letter, much folded. Kemp had found it beneath his daughter's pillow. He handed it over with an expression I can only describe as disgust, and Holmes read it twice and passed it to me.

It was written in pencil, in a careful, laboured hand — the hand of someone who had been taught to write and had not been given much occasion to practise. It was signed *Ada*. The sense of it was plain enough: that the writer had taken a room; that Nell was to come to her in the small hours of the Sunday night, while the house slept towards the holiday; that she was to bring nothing and tell nobody. There was one line I read twice at the time and have not been able to put out of my head since.

*You are not obliged to stay in that house because he is your father.*

"Ada," said Holmes.

"Ada Wrenn. She was a housemaid of mine until the spring." Kemp's voice had changed. The tremor was still in his hands but it had got into his throat as well, and what came out of him now was harder than grief. "I turned her off for theft. I could not prove it — money went from a drawer, and there was no one else it could have been — but I could not prove it, and so I did not have her taken up, and I have regretted that mercy every hour since this morning. She has taken my daughter. She has filled that girl's head for a year with God knows what, and now she has taken her, and I will see her arrested. I will see her hanged for it."

"Will you," said Holmes mildly.

"I —" The man stopped. Something went out of him. He sat down at last, on the edge of the basket chair, and put his hands over his face. "I do not want the police in my house, Mr. Holmes. Do you understand me? This is a family matter. My family's matter. If it goes to the police it goes to the newspapers, and I will not have my daughter's name — " He broke off. "I want her found. I want her brought home. That is all I want. The rest is talk. I have been talking to myself all day and I have said a great many things I did not mean."

I have set down that contradiction exactly as it came, because I noticed it at the time and thought it did him credit rather than otherwise: a man raging that he would see a girl hanged, and in the same breath refusing to summon a constable. Grief is not a consistent creature. I have watched it in field hospitals and in drawing rooms and it has never once behaved logically.

Holmes asked him a number of small questions then — the hours the household kept, the position of the garden wall, whether Nell had money of her own, whether she had been in any way altered in her manner of late.

"Altered," said Kemp slowly. "She has been unwell. Not ill enough for a doctor. Only — in the mornings. This last fortnight, or three weeks. She has been sick in the mornings and well enough by noon, and she would not have me send for anybody, and I did not press her."

The room was very quiet.

I did not look at Holmes and Holmes did not look at me, and I am certain that neither of us altered his expression by so much as a degree. But I knew, in the way one knows these things after long acquaintance, that we had arrived at precisely the same place at precisely the same moment, and that neither of us was going to say so in front of that man.

"Thank you," said Holmes. "That is useful."

He returned the shoe to the bag, and kept the letter.

"I shall come to your house at nine tomorrow morning with Dr. Watson, and I shall examine the garden, the wall, and your daughter's room. Until then you will do nothing. You will visit no more lodging houses. You will go home and you will eat something and you will sleep."

"I shall not sleep."

"Then you will lie down, which will serve nearly as well." Holmes took the man's hand briefly at the door. "Mr. Kemp. Your daughter left by a window, in the company of a person who had written to arrange it. That is not an abduction. Whatever else it may prove to be, it is not that, and you may take what comfort you can from the distinction."

Kemp went down the stairs like a man who has been given permission to stop carrying something. I heard the street door, and his step on the pavement, and then nothing.

### III. After

Holmes did not speak for some minutes. He had the shoe out of the bag again and was turning it under the lamp.

"Well, Watson. Your patient."

"He is not my patient."

"He is anybody's patient who has eyes. Give me your reading of him."

"He drinks," I said. "Heavily, and for years — you saw the face, and the colour of him. And he has taken nothing today, which is why he could not sit still and could not hold his hat and would not have the brandy when I offered it. The sweat and the shaking are withdrawal, nothing more. I have seen it in a hundred men. By tomorrow he will be worse, and the day after he will be better, and then he will begin again."

"And the sickness in the mornings?"

"The girl is with child," I said. "A fortnight to three weeks of it, on his account, which puts her further on than that — a father notices late. I am sorry for him. He has not the least idea."

"No," said Holmes, "he has not." He set the shoe down and looked into the fire that was not lit. "Consider it from her side. Seventeen years old, in the house of a man who told us in this room that he would see a servant hanged for a theft he could not prove. What does such a girl do when she finds herself in that condition? She does not tell him. She runs, if she has anywhere to run to — and a fortnight ago she had nowhere, and then a letter arrived saying *I have got the room*."

"Then it is a kindness," I said. "Whatever Kemp believes of her. Ada Wrenn has done that girl a kindness."

"It has that appearance." Holmes turned the letter over. "I dislike the arithmetic of it, all the same. A housemaid dismissed in March, without a character, on an accusation of theft. Four months later she has a room of her own, a wage sufficient to keep two, and a plan laid to the hour. Girls turned off without a character do not commonly land so softly."

"And the shoe?"

"The shoe is the one honest thing in the bag." He held it out to me. "Smell it."

I did. Beneath the leather and the mud there was something else — faint, but once caught, impossible to lose. A thin, sharp, garlic reek.

"Phosphorus," said Holmes. "She works in a match factory, or has done recently enough for it to be in her clothes. It gets into everything, they tell me — the hair, the skin, the bedding. You cannot wash it out, and after a while you cannot smell it upon yourself at all." He tossed the shoe back into the bag. "Which answers my arithmetic. A girl put out of a good house in March has spent her summer dipping frames for eleven shillings a week, and has contrived out of eleven shillings a week to rescue somebody. I have known worse people, Watson."

I said something to the effect that it was a straightforward enough business, then, and that we should likely have the girl home by Wednesday, and the young man found and spoken to, and the whole thing settled with as little noise as such things ever are.

I record my own words here as faithfully as I am able. I have found it a useful discipline to keep an exact account of the occasions upon which I have been comprehensively wrong.

## SCENE TWO — THE FATHER'S HOUSE

### IV. The Morning

**Tuesday, 7th August. Denmark Hill.**

The blinds were down at the Kemp house when we arrived, every one of them, and there was a doctor's gig standing in the drive, and I knew before we were out of the cab.

Holmes went up the steps two at a time. I have rarely seen him move so quickly for a man he had met once.

The housekeeper let us in — a Mrs. Ince, grey and upright and entirely composed, in the way of women who have run a house for twenty years and expect to go on running it whatever has happened upstairs. The doctor met us on the landing: a tired, decent man named Furnivall, who had a practice in Camberwell Grove and had been fetched out of his breakfast. He was already writing.

"Kemp, Edward. Fifty-one. Found by the housekeeper at half past seven. He is cold; I should put it somewhere in the small hours, three or four o'clock, though I would not wish to be held to it." He glanced at Holmes without much interest. "Heart. It is nearly always heart, at that age and that build. And I am told his daughter went missing yesterday morning and he walked half of London looking for her, which will not have helped him. Strain and a bad heart and a bottle. I have seen a hundred of them."

"He was not your patient?"

"He was nobody's patient, sir, which is rather the trouble. He would not have a doctor to it." Furnivall's patience was thinning. "Mrs. Ince tells me he has been failing since the spring — off his food, off his colour, tired at his desk — and he is yellow as a guinea, which tells me the rest. I shall send a line to the coroner's officer, as I am obliged to, and the coroner's officer will send it back to me, as he always does, because a gentleman who drank found dead in his own bed is not a matter the coroner of this district sits on in August. There is no wound. There is no sign of violence. Nothing is disturbed. He is lying in his own bed in his own nightshirt with his own blankets drawn up. What precisely am I to be suspicious of?"

"I do not know yet," said Holmes. "That is rather the difficulty."

The bedroom was as the doctor had described it. Kemp lay on his back with his head turned a little to the left, and his face had the colour I had noted in the lamplight at Baker Street, deepened now to something frankly jaundiced. His hands were outside the covers. There was no disorder of any kind.

On the writing desk beneath the window stood a spirit decanter, three-quarters empty, and a used tumbler beside it with perhaps a mouthful dried in the bottom.

On the night table, within reach of the dead man's hand, stood a water carafe and a glass.

Holmes looked at the desk for perhaps two seconds. He looked at the night table for rather longer.

"Watson. Last night you told me this man would not sleep."

"I did."

"He appears to have managed it." Holmes crouched until his eyes were level with the carafe and did not touch it. "Note the level. It is barely half full, and the water has a film upon it, and there is a ring dried on the glass a good inch above where it stands now. That water was not drawn last night. It has been standing since the night before at least. And yet a man in the state you described does not go a night without drinking. He pours himself three glasses, and does not trouble with the stopper after the first."

> `[GAME]` EXAMINE the carafe and the decanter. These are observations only; the test is not available yet.

"Holmes —"

"And note, if you please, that the doctor is entirely correct. There is nothing in this room to be suspicious of." He rose. "That is what I object to. Every room I have ever entered has had something in it. Let us go and look at the daughter's."

### V. Nell's Room

The room told its story by what it withheld as much as by what it offered.

The bed was made, or nearly — the coverlet drawn up with the particular carelessness of one who has made it a thousand mornings and will not break the habit even in flight. The window had been shut by somebody since, and the flower bed two storeys below still showed the mark where the shoe had lain, and beside it two square dents in the soil a ladder's width apart. Of the girl herself the room said almost nothing. A brush. A few books, none of them recent. A wardrobe with gaps in it where garments had been taken in haste. The grate was swept and cold; there had been no fire laid in that room, Mrs. Ince told us, since May.

Holmes gave all of it perhaps a minute. He went instead to the washstand, where a tin box of household matches sat beside the basin.

He opened it and looked in, and then held it out to me without a word.

There were no matches in it. There were a few loose match-heads in the bottom, scraped from their sticks, and above them, packed close, a nest of small papers, each folded twice and twisted shut and then untwisted again, and every one of them empty.

"Count them, if you would be so good."

I did. There were eighteen.

"Eighteen," said Holmes. "And Easter fell on the first of April." He was very still. "Eighteen Fridays, Watson, from the week after Easter to last Friday, if a twist of paper came into this house each week. In a room with no fire in it to burn them, and in a house where she dared not leave them in the slop pail for Mrs. Ince to find."

He lifted a chipped teacup from beside the basin — rinsed, but not scoured — and held it beneath his nose, and then passed it to me.

I caught it at once. Fainter here than it had been upon the leather, but by then I had learned the smell and I could not have missed it.

"The same as the shoe," I said.

"The same as the shoe." He set the cup down very carefully, as though it had become fragile. "And I have been a fool, Watson, in a manner I shall want some time to forgive. I stood in our rooms last night with that smell in my hand and I congratulated myself upon having traced a housemaid to her place of employment."

He had been turning the papers over as he spoke, one after another, as a man deals cards he does not mean to play, and at the fourteenth or fifteenth he stopped.

It was not like the others. They were cut from sugar-bags and newspaper; this was a strip from a printed form, torn across, with a line of ink still on it in a landlady's round hand. *Recd. of Miss Wrenn, 2s. 9d., one week* — and then, beneath, the half of an address: a court off St. Leonard's Street, Bromley.

"She tore it off whatever she had to hand," said Holmes, "because paper costs money and she had none to spare, and because it did not occur to her for one moment that anybody in this house would ever look." He folded it into his pocketbook. "I am obliged to her. It is the only piece of luck I have had in this affair, and I did nothing whatever to earn it."

### VI. The Water Glass

We returned to the father's room, and it was there that certainty, or the beginning of it, arrived.

Holmes drew from his coat the small spirit lamp he carries on those occasions when he suspects a chemical answer waiting behind a domestic question, and a shallow dish, and set them both into my hands.

"You will want the curtains drawn," he said, "and the gas out. Pour off a little — a little — and hold it over the flame, and watch the surface of the water, not the flame. You are a medical man. You will know what you are looking at when you see it, and I would rather it were you who saw it first."

I did as he told me. I drew the curtains close and put out the gas, so that we stood in near darkness but for the small blue flame, and I decanted a little of the water into the dish, and held it above the lamp.

> `[GAME]` USE the spirit lamp WITH the carafe. Available only once the matchbox papers have been counted.

I will set down plainly what I saw, though I should not credit it from another man's pen.

As the water warmed, a faint greenish luminescence rose from the surface — a cold, ghostly light, gone almost as soon as it came, but unmistakable to any man who has read his Mitscherlich. It is not the proper test, which wants a still and a condenser and a laboratory dark; it is a crude cousin of it. But I have read the cases, and I knew what I was looking at.

Holmes tried the tumbler from the writing desk afterwards, and the spirits in it gave nothing.

"Phosphorus," he said quietly, and there was none of the satisfaction in it that usually attends his naming of a thing. "Scraped from match-heads, crushed, and left to stand in water. Not in his brandy, Watson. In the water he takes to bed every night of his life because of the brandy. The one vessel in this house that a man of his habits was certain to empty, and to fill again, and to think nothing of."

"How long?" I asked him. "How long would a dose take to kill a man?"

"You are asking the wrong question, and I asked it myself for a quarter of an hour this morning before those papers corrected me." He nodded at the bed. "Listen to the doctor. *Failing since the spring. Off his food, off his colour, tired at his desk.* Listen to yourself, last night, diagnosing a drunkard by the colour of his face. Listen to the man himself telling us he has eaten nothing and could not say when he last did. This did not begin on Sunday. This began the week after Easter and it has gone on in small quantities ever since, a little at a time in a carafe that was filled every evening, and every symptom of it has been swallowed whole by the fact that Edward Kemp was a heavy drinker with a ruined liver. There is no better mask in England. The poison and the vice produce the same yellow face."

"And this?" I said. "The last of it?"

"On Sunday night she put into the carafe whatever she had left, because after Sunday night she would not be in the house to do it again. He drank from it when the commotion woke him, and was sick on his own terrace, and laid it to the shock. He walked half of London on it. He came to us at nine o'clock with a sweat upon him and a tremor in his hands, and I watched you diagnose him correctly, and neither of us was within a mile of the truth." He was looking at the dead man's face. "And then he came home, and lay down as I told him to, and reached for his water. Nobody had carried up fresh. It was not anybody's office in this house but hers. He drank what she had left him."

I did not answer. I was thinking of the brandy I had offered him, and of how little good it would have done him either way.

"Do not," said Holmes, without turning round. "You examined a man with every symptom of withdrawal and you concluded that he suffered from withdrawal. It was the correct diagnosis. It was simply not the only true thing about him."

He took a small phial from his case, filled it from the carafe, stoppered it, and put it in his pocket without labelling it.

When we came down Mrs. Ince had already sent for the undertaker and was setting the house to rights, as such women do, because there is nothing else to be done with a morning like that one. As we waited in the hall for the cab I saw the girl from the scullery carry the carafe down past us, rinsed and upturned upon a tray.

Holmes watched it go by, and said nothing at all.

## SCENE THREE — THE ROOM

### VII. The Court

**Tuesday, 7th August. Bromley, the afternoon.**

The court stood off St. Leonard's Street, at the Bromley end, three storeys of soot and damp with the marsh smell coming up behind it and the smell of the match works over everything, so that one tasted the place rather than breathed it.

Bromley had heard the news before we had. A woman was shelling peas upon a step at the mouth of the court, and two more stood over her, and the word they were passing between them was *George Yard*.

Holmes stopped, and asked, as one might ask a stranger the hour.

"Woman on the stairs at George Yard Buildings, sir, Whitechapel way. Found her before five this morning. Stabbed — " the speaker looked at me and then at Holmes and chose her word — "a great many times. Thirty and more, they're saying." She shrugged, with the confidence of a woman repeating what the whole street knew. "Soldiers, most likely, it being the bank holiday. Nobody knows who she is. Nobody's come forward. These things happen down that way."

"No doubt," said Holmes, and thanked her.

He said nothing more of it until we were at the foot of the stair. Then, low, and more to himself than to me:

"A woman with a great many wounds on a public stair, and all of Whitechapel talking of her by noon. A man with no wound at all, in his own bed in Camberwell, and not a soul in London talking of him anywhere." He looked up the dark stairwell. "I wonder, Watson, whether London's attention is always so reliably drawn to the louder crime."

I did not think much of it at the time. It was, as the woman said, a rough sort of place, and these things happened down that way, and we had a poisoning of our own and a missing girl of seventeen.

I record it here only because I have since learned to distrust anything Holmes calls unremarkable in that particular tone of voice.

The stairs were narrow and the landings dark at noon. On the top floor there was a single door, and behind it, faintly, the sound of somebody moving about.

Holmes did not knock in his usual manner. He stood a moment with his hand raised, and then he lowered it, and said to me, very low:

"Whatever is behind this door, Watson, I should be grateful if you would go through it first."

I have known Sherlock Holmes to walk unarmed into a room containing a man with a revolver. He did not wish to be the first face those two saw, and he knew perfectly well why, and I confess it moved me more than a great many braver things he has done.

I knocked, and gave my name and my profession, and said that nobody was going to be taken anywhere.

The bolt went back after a long time.

The woman who opened it was perhaps four-and-twenty, thin, with her sleeves pushed up and her hair tied out of the way of work. Her left cheek was swollen along the line of the jaw, and she touched it once while she looked at us, in the unconscious way one touches a pain that has stopped being news. When she spoke I saw that a tooth was gone on that side, and that the gum about the gap had a grey, dead look that I had seen before, in the out-patients' room at Bart's, in the mouths of women from the match works.

I knew what that was, or thought I did. In other women it is the work of years. In her it had been months, and something had helped it in.

Behind her, sitting up on a mattress against the wall with a blanket about her shoulders, was a girl with her father's colouring and none of his weight, so plainly seventeen years old that I felt something turn over in my chest.

"He said a doctor," said Ada Wrenn. "You're from him."

"No," said Holmes, from the landing. "We were."

### VIII. What Ada Said

She put herself in front of the mattress. It was not a dramatic gesture; she simply moved, the way one moves a chair into a doorway, and stood there with her hands at her sides.

"Whatever it is, I did it," she said. "Say what it is and I'll say I did it. She's not to be in it."

"Miss Wrenn —"

"I wrote to her and I fetched a ladder from the yard next door and I took her out of that window, and I lost my shoe in the flower bed doing it, and if he's told you I stole from him he told that in March and he was a liar then and he's a liar now." Her voice was climbing. "So say what it is."

"Edward Kemp is dead," said Holmes.

I had thought I knew what would follow. I had prepared myself, upon the stairs, for a scene of the kind one reads about — the shriek, the collapse, the confession torn out by shock.

What happened was that Ada Wrenn said, "Good," and then put her hand over her mouth as though the word had got out past her guard, and behind her the girl upon the mattress did not move at all.

"He died in his bed," Holmes went on, "in the small hours of this morning, of what an entirely competent physician has recorded as a failure of the heart. The coroner's officer will pass it, as he passes a hundred like it. There will be no inquest. There is at this moment not one man in London with the slightest interest in the matter, and I will tell you frankly that there is unlikely ever to be, because London today has other things to look at." He took off his hat. "I am not here on behalf of the police. I am here because Dr. Watson warmed the water from his carafe over a spirit lamp, and it burned green."

The silence went on for some time.

"It was mine," said Ada. "The whole of it was mine. I had by then formed a complete and coherent account of the business, and I offer it here without softening, because I think it is the account that any decent man in that room would have formed, and because I was wrong in every particular.

Here was a girl in trouble, in the house of a hard father who would not have borne it. It seemed to me, further, that there was a young man somewhere in London who had not been asked for anything, and who might yet be made to answer, and that this was a question better put by a physician than by a detective.

## THE PAGE

### X. The Page I Have Not Finished

**Tuesday, 7th August. Baker Street, night.**

> `[GAME]` A scene cut back to 221B. The diary page is where the choice is made.

There is no case here unless somebody makes one.

Edward Kemp will be buried on Friday under a certificate signed by a competent man who found precisely what he expected to find. The coroner's officer has his note and will send it back. The carafe has been rinsed by a scullery girl who knew nothing of any of it, and the papers in the matchbox mean nothing to anybody who has not smelled the teacup. What remains is a phial of water, stoppered and unlabelled, in Holmes's chemical cabinet, where he put it this evening without a word and has not looked at it since. It would be enough. A letter from me to the coroner would put it in the hands of the Home Office analyst by the end of the week, and the analyst would find in it what my lamp found, and there would be an exhumation and an inquest and the rest of it.

Set against that: a girl of seventeen who will be delivered in the spring, and a woman of four-and-twenty whose jaw is rotting out of her face because she said a true thing to a man who struck her for it and then sent her back to the works without a character.

If I write, they are both taken. Nell Kemp will be tried, and her father's reputation will be defended by counsel at her expense, since he is not here to be asked about it and she is. Every particular of what was done to her in that house will be read out and printed and sold, and she will be delivered in Newgate or somewhere very like it.

If I do not write, then two people who between them ended a man's life will go on living, and I shall have decided upon my own authority, with no commission from anybody, that this was right.

Holmes has said almost nothing. On the stairs at Bromley he told me that the law and justice are frequently the same thing and that a man is a fool who assumes it, and since then he has sat in his chair with his eyes shut and left the matter where he plainly intends it to stay, which is with me. I asked him once, directly, what he would do. He said that he had already done it — that he had gone into that room believing he was pursuing a servant who had stolen a child, and that he had come out of it having got the whole of it exactly backwards, and that a man who has been that wrong before noon has not earned the right to sentence anybody before supper.

There is a woman on a stair in George Yard whom nobody has yet claimed. I mention it only because I caught myself thinking, as I came up the stairs just now, that there is a great deal of London and only a certain quantity of attention to go round, and that a good many things must pass through the gaps.

I do not yet know whether I intend that as a comfort or as an accusation.

The page is not finished. I find I cannot finish it until I have decided what it is that I am going to write.

## EPILOGUE A — CLOSE THE DIARY

**Saturday, 18th August.**

> `[GAME — VERBATIM]` Fixed authored text, not played. Shown on the "Close the diary" branch.

We went down to the house once more, a week and a half afterwards, at Holmes's suggestion and for a reason he did not give me until we were at the gate.

"I wish to see what she does with it," he said. "A man may be judged by his crimes, Watson. I have generally found that a person is better judged by what they do on the first ordinary morning afterwards."

The estate had passed to her entire, to be held for her until she came of age by a solicitor in Gray's Inn who had known the family twenty years. He had expressed, I gathered, no surprise whatever at the manner of Edward Kemp's death, and rather more at the fact that his daughter was still alive to inherit; and he appeared to regard his trusteeship as a matter of signing whatever she put in front of him.

The door was opened to us by Ada Wrenn, in a black dress and a clean apron, with her hair up.

She showed us into the morning room precisely as a housemaid shows two callers into a morning room. There was nothing insolent in it. That was, I think, what took the breath out of me: the sheer, immaculate, deliberate ordinariness of it — the woman who had been turned off that house in March for saying a true thing to its master, standing in its hall in its uniform, holding its door.

Nell Kemp received us by the window. She had colour in her face for the first time since I had seen her. She thanked us both for coming, and asked after Mrs. Hudson, whose name she had somehow retained, and poured the tea herself.

"You will want to know what we intend," she said.

"I confess to some curiosity," said Holmes.

"I intend to live here." She said it without any defiance at all, which made it a great deal worse. "It is my house. There is nowhere else in England that Ada can be paid a wage and sleep behind a bolted door and have a surgeon to her jaw before it takes the rest of her face, and she will not go to one until somebody makes her. And there is nowhere else that I can have this child in the spring and not be spat at in the street for it." She looked at me. "The talk will be that the master's daughter has kept on a girl her father dismissed, which is an odd sort of Christian charity, and people will think me soft and they will be perfectly satisfied with that explanation. Nobody has ever needed more than a dull explanation, Dr. Watson. I learned that in this house a long time ago."

Holmes set down his cup.

"You understand that Dr. Watson and I will know."

"Yes."

"And that we may at any time cease to be so accommodating."

"Yes," said Nell Kemp. "I have thought about that a great deal, and I find I do not spend very much of my time frightened of two men who took their hats off in that room in Bromley. I may be wrong. I have been wrong about a man's character before." She poured him more tea. "But you have had a week and a half, sir, and the coroner has not called."

There was nothing to be said to that, and Holmes, to his credit, did not attempt anything.

We walked as far as the corner before either of us spoke, and it was I who broke it, and what I said was that I did not know whether we had just been thanked or warned.

"Both," said Holmes. "And with better manners than I should have managed at seventeen." He stopped to light his pipe and did not succeed in the wind. "It is finished, Watson, and I am obliged to record that I did nothing whatever to finish it. I identified a poison and I traced a shoe, and I understood not one material fact about that family until a girl on a mattress in Bromley told me in four words, and the meaning of the four words was in a letter I had been carrying in my coat since the Monday night."

"You could not have known."

"I could have considered it." He gave up on the pipe. "I constructed a story out of the pieces on the table and it was a good story and every piece fitted it, and it was wrong, and it was wrong in the one direction I did not look because I did not care to look there. Remember that, when the next one comes. The evidence will always fit the tale you have already decided to tell. That is not a property of the evidence."

## EPILOGUE B — WRITE TO THE CORONER

**Saturday, 18th August.**

> `[GAME — VERBATIM]` Fixed authored text, not played. Shown on the "Write to the coroner" branch. **First draft by Claude; for the author's rewrite.**

I wrote the letter on the Wednesday morning, before Holmes was up, and took it to the post myself so that I should not have the night to think better of it.

The coroner's officer called on the Thursday: a slow, civil man with a notebook, who had plainly expected a crank and was disconcerted to find two. Holmes received him in his dressing-gown, answered every question put to him in as few words as the English language permits, and at the end of it went to his cabinet and gave the man the phial without a word. He did not look at me while he did it. He has not reproached me since, by so much as a glance, and I have come to understand that this is the reproach.

The rest went as I had written that it would go. It went faster. The analyst had his answer by the Monday. There was an order on the Tuesday, and the thing was done at Nunhead on the Wednesday morning, very early, with screens up.

They were taken together, from the room in Bromley, on the Thursday, and by the Friday the newspapers had it, though not yet the half of it. The half of it will come at the trial. I was not there. I am told that Ada Wrenn said again that it was all hers and the girl was not to be in it, and said it to every man in turn until they put her in the van; and that Nell Kemp said nothing at all, and went down the stairs on her own feet, and asked whether somebody might be sent to Mrs. Ince to say she would not be home.

On the Saturday I went down to Denmark Hill alone. I do not know what I meant to do there. The blinds were down still, or down again, and there was straw laid in the road before the house, as there is for an invalid, though there was nobody in it now to be disturbed. Mrs. Ince was on the area steps, and saw me, and went inside, and shut the door.

There was a letter for me at Baker Street when I came back. It had come through the coroner's officer, and it was in pencil, in a round, careful, schoolroom hand I had never seen before and knew at once.

*Dr. Watson. You took your hat off. I wanted you to know that I noticed. N. K.*

I have put it in this book, facing the page I did finish, and I have not yet been able to decide which of the two I find the harder to look at.

Holmes came with me as far as the corner of Baker Street that evening, which he does not commonly do, and said one thing only before he turned back.

"You did what the law asks of a man, Watson. I have spent a week trying to discover what else it might have asked, and I find I cannot tell you. That is not a comfort. I did not intend it as one."
