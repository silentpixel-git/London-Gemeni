## The story

It had been a close and airless bank holiday, the sort of day that empties London of everyone who can afford to be emptied and leaves the rest of us to sit in our shirtsleeves and resent them. By half past seven both windows of our sitting room stood open to Baker Street, and the holiday came up off the pavement in waves: barrel-organs, a brass band somewhere towards the Park, and under it all the long, unbroken murmur of a hundred thousand people with nothing to do and nowhere they were obliged to be.

Holmes had spent the afternoon in a chemical silence. It had ended a little after seven, when something on his bench went black and stank, and since then he had been at the window with his hands behind him, looking down upon the crowd as a naturalist looks into a pond in which nothing of interest has bred.

"London has taken a holiday from wickedness, Watson," he said, without turning. "I find I resent it more than I can easily express."

"Most men would call that a cause for celebration."

"Most men have occupations." He nodded down into the street. "Look at them. Not one of them is doing anything that requires the smallest explanation. The licensed victualler by the pillar box has kept his daughter out an hour past the time her mother allowed, and will be made to pay for it. The man under the lamp walked in from Hampstead this morning to save the fare, and has spent it since on something he already regrets. There is the whole of the street's mystery, and I have had it since six."

"You cannot possibly know that he walked."

"I observe that his boots are white to the ankle with the dust of the Heath road. That is a fact. I infer that he walked, which is not, though I should be very much surprised to be wrong." He glanced at me at last. "You will note the difference between those two things, Watson, because nobody else in London does."

I said, with some feeling, that it was a very good parlour trick, and that any man might do it who had nothing else to do with his evening.

It was a mistake, and I knew it as it left my mouth. Holmes turned from the window with an expression I had learned, in seven years, to distrust: the look of a man who has been offered a diversion and does not intend to let it go.

"Any man," he said. "Very well. A wager. I shall choose two people in that street, and you shall read them for me — who they are, what they have been doing, what they are about. Read them both correctly and I give you my word that I shall not touch the violin before midnight."

That was a prize worth having. He had been at the violin, on and off, since Saturday.

"And if I fail?"

"Then you shall sit in that chair and read my monograph upon the ashes of the various tobaccos, all hundred and forty varieties of it, and you shall not skip the plates." He gestured at the window as a man holds open a door. "The old fellow on the kerb by the cab rank. And the clergyman outside the chemist's. Take your time. I have nothing whatever else to do."

---

I took the old man first, since he seemed the easier.

He was perhaps sixty, in a decent coat too heavy for August, and he stood at the kerb as though somebody had told him to stand there and had not yet told him to stop: heels together, chin up, a stick in his left hand that he leaned on rather more than a man of his years would wish to. When he moved to let a perambulator by, I saw how he carried the left leg — a short, stiff swing from the hip, the knee hardly bending at all — and I knew that walk. I had watched it learned in the hospital at Peshawar, by men who had been told they would keep the leg and had not been told what keeping it would cost them.

His neck, below the line of a cap he no longer wore, was burned a deep brick-colour that no English summer gives a man, and a fortnight of this one had not touched it.

"A soldier," I said. "An infantryman, by the way he stands; cavalrymen stand otherwise. He has served in India, and not lately — the colour is old, and has gone into the skin. And he was wounded in the left thigh, a fracture, set by a regimental surgeon in a hurry, and healed short. He has not been out of his regiment long enough to stop standing to attention when he is waiting for something. A sergeant, I should think. He has the look of one."

Holmes was silent for a moment.

"Admirable," he said, and I believe he meant it. "Every word of it. You have seen what I saw, and you have seen the leg better than I could, which is your profession and not mine. I should only have added that he is waiting for a grandchild, because he keeps looking at the height of a child's head in the crowd and not at the height of a man's. One to you, Watson."

I confess I was pleased with myself, and turned to the clergyman with some confidence.

He stood outside the chemist's in a long black coat and a white tie, with a small book open in his hand, and he was plainly not a prosperous man: the coat was shiny at the seams, the cuffs had been turned, and the hat was a good one gone to the bad. He was reading the book with his lips moving, and now and then he looked up from it and away over the heads of the crowd, as a man does who is repeating something to himself.

"A curate," I said. "A poor parish, by his coat — somewhere in the country, I should think, since he has come up for the holiday and looks as though he does not much care for it. He is getting tomorrow's lesson by heart. A conscientious man, and an underpaid one."

"Ah," said Holmes, very gently, and handed me his opera glass from the mantel. "Look again. Not at his coat — you have done his coat very thoroughly. Look at the line of his hair, beside the ear, and at the book."

I looked. Along the hairline, where a careless towel had missed it, there was a faint brownish line upon the skin, too even to be dirt. The book was not a prayer-book. It was a thin paper volume, much thumbed, with a coloured wrapper, and the lines in it were set out in the short, uneven lengths that no psalm was ever printed in.

"Greasepaint," I said, and felt foolish.

"Greasepaint. He is an actor, between the matinée and the evening performance at the Princess's in Oxford Street, which I should guess is a melodrama with a clergyman in it, because he has not troubled to change. He is going over his lines because he has been given new ones today and does not trust them." He took the glass back. "You saw a black coat and a white tie, and you stopped looking, because you had your answer. Everybody does it. It is the commonest error in the world, and the most expensive. One to me."

> **Branch — the player DID use the glass on the clergyman first:**

I had taken Holmes's opera glass from the mantel before I gave my answer, and it was as well that I did. Along the man's hairline, beside the ear, there was a faint brownish line upon the skin, too even to be dirt; and the book was not a prayer-book at all, but a thin paper volume in a coloured wrapper, with the lines set out in short, uneven lengths.

"Or," I said slowly, "a man who would like you to think so. He is no curate, Holmes. That is greasepaint at his hairline, and he is learning a part, not a lesson. He is an actor between performances, and he has not changed out of his costume."

Holmes looked at me for so long that I began to fear I had made some fresh error.

"The Princess's, in Oxford Street," he said at last. "A melodrama with a clergyman in it. You have it exactly." He sat down rather heavily in his chair. "I begin to think I shall have to find another occupation."

> **Shared from here.**

The light was going. The lamplighter had come down the far side of the street, and the gas was up in the windows opposite, and the crowd below had thickened rather than thinned, as holiday crowds do when the public-houses begin to be the only places still open.

Holmes had gone back to the window.

"You did very well, Watson," he said. "Better than you know. But I will tell you the thing that struck me while you were at work, and it is not a comfortable thing." He was looking down into the street, at nothing in particular. "We chose those two because they were remarkable. The soldier by his stance, the actor by his coat. Each of them did something to catch the eye. Now — how many people have passed under this window since you began?"

"I have not the least idea. Some hundreds."

"Some hundreds. Describe one of them to me. One. Any one you like, that I did not point out to you."

I tried. I could not do it. I could recall the general sense of them — straw hats, a great many children, a sailor — but not one face, not one coat, not one particular man.

"Nor could I," said Holmes. "Nor could any constable on any beat in London. We see what asks to be seen. A man who asks nothing of anybody — who dresses as the street dresses and walks as the street walks — might pass under this window every evening for a year and never once be looked at." He let the curtain fall. "It is the whole of my complaint against this city, Watson, and I have never found a remedy for it."

I said that it was hardly a complaint at all, on a holiday evening, and that London might be forgiven a little dullness once a year.

He smiled at that, and did not answer.

> **Branch — Watson won the wager:** Holmes took up the violin from its case, looked at it, and put it down again; and he kept his word until midnight, which was more than I had dared to expect.
>
> **Branch — Watson lost the wager:** Holmes took down the monograph upon the ashes of the various tobaccos and laid it in my lap with great tenderness, and I read it, plates and all, until the street was quiet.

I have thought of that evening a good deal since. The windows stood open until past midnight, and the noise of the holiday went on below them, warm and foolish and entirely harmless, and neither of us thought to close them. It was, I believe, the last evening of that summer on which it did not occur to me to do so.

**Why the glass works as a tutorial device:** the player is never told to use it. The hint system can steer them ("the clergyman is a long way off"), but a player who reaches for the tool first is rewarded with Watson's best moment. A player who doesn't is corrected by Holmes, and that teaches the act's lesson: *you saw a black coat and stopped looking*. Both outcomes teach something, and neither blocks progress.

## Notes and open questions

1. **What's reused:** the victualler, his daughter, the Hampstead man and the Heath-road dust all already exist in `facts.ts` and the `act0_caller_noticed` story event, with topics. The demonstration beat can keep most of that. Everything to do with Mrs Kemp, the pawn ticket, the boots, the workbox, the letters and the charity card goes. **This breaks saves.**
2. **Foreshadowing:** Holmes's "a man who asks nothing of anybody… never once be looked at" is the game's thesis about the killer, stated in August before anything has happened. It doesn't hint at a murder, but it is deliberate dramatic irony. It's your call whether that's too pointed for the current Act 0 rule ("nothing may be hinted at"). The softer version cuts the last sentence of Holmes's speech.
3. **The last line of the coda** ("the last evening… on which it did not occur to me to [close the windows]") is the only darkness in the act. Keep it or cut it.
4. **The bridge to Act 1:** `ACT_BRIDGES[1]` currently starts "Our night's vigil had availed nothing…", left over from the old prologue. It needs rewriting to fit, whatever Act 1 turns out to be (currently Miller's Court, 9 Nov).
5. **History:** the Princess's Theatre, Oxford Street, was running in 1888. Watson's Afghan service (Peshawar base hospital) and the seven years of friendship (they met in 1881) are both canon. Holmes smokes a pipe; the violin is canon. Nothing here is set after August 1888.
